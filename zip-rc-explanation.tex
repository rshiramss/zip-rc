\begin{abstract}
ZIP-RC is a simple but clever framework that teaches language models to predict two things while generating text: (1) ``Am I going to get this right?'' and (2) ``How much longer until I'm done?'' The key insight is that these predictions come for \textbf{free}---no extra computation needed---by adding just 10 special tokens to the vocabulary. This document explains how it works in plain terms.
\end{abstract}

\section{What Problem Does ZIP-RC Solve?}
%==============================================================================

When a language model generates text, it's essentially ``flying blind.'' It has no idea:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Is my current approach leading to a correct answer?
    \item How many more tokens do I need to generate?
\end{itemize}

ZIP-RC fixes both problems by teaching the model to make predictions about its own output---at every single step of generation.

\subsection{The Core Idea}

Train the model to predict two things at every step:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reward}: Will I get this right? (yes or no)
    \item \textbf{Length}: How many more tokens until I'm done? (rough estimate)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why This Matters}

Once the model can predict these, you can make smart decisions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low confidence?} Stop early and try a different approach
    \item \textbf{Many tokens left + low success?} Don't waste compute on a doomed path
    \item \textbf{Generating multiple options?} Pick the one most likely to be correct
\end{itemize}

\section{The ZIP Token System}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{The Basic Setup}

ZIP-RC adds 10 special tokens to the model's vocabulary. These tokens represent combinations of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{2 reward bins}: wrong (0) or correct (1)
    \item \textbf{5 length bins}: roughly how many tokens remain
\end{itemize}

That's $2 \times 5 = 10$ ZIP tokens total.

\subsection{How Length Binning Works}

The remaining tokens are grouped into 5 bins:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ccp{5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Bin} & \textbf{Tokens Left} & \textbf{Plain English} \\
\midrule
0 & 0--1 & Almost done (just finishing up) \\
1 & 2--3 & A few tokens left \\
2 & 4--7 & Several tokens left \\
3 & 8--15 & Quite a few tokens left \\
4 & 16+ & Lots of tokens left \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Why exponential bins?} Because the difference between 2 and 10 tokens matters more than the difference between 50 and 100. Early predictions need finer resolution.

\textbf{Example:} If there are 5 tokens left, that falls in bin 2 (the 4--7 range).

\subsection{The 10 ZIP Tokens}

Each ZIP token combines a reward prediction with a length prediction:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}clp{6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Token} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{When to use} \\
\midrule
\ziptoken{0} & Wrong + almost done & Wrong answer, 0--1 tokens left \\
\ziptoken{1} & Wrong + few left & Wrong answer, 2--3 tokens left \\
\ziptoken{2} & Wrong + several left & Wrong answer, 4--7 tokens left \\
\ziptoken{3} & Wrong + many left & Wrong answer, 8--15 tokens left \\
\ziptoken{4} & Wrong + lots left & Wrong answer, 16+ tokens left \\
\ziptoken{5} & Correct + almost done & Correct answer, 0--1 tokens left \\
\ziptoken{6} & Correct + few left & Correct answer, 2--3 tokens left \\
\ziptoken{7} & Correct + several left & Correct answer, 4--7 tokens left \\
\ziptoken{8} & Correct + many left & Correct answer, 8--15 tokens left \\
\ziptoken{9} & Correct + lots left & Correct answer, 16+ tokens left \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Simple Formula}

To figure out which ZIP token to use:
\[
\text{ZIP token number} = (\text{reward bin}) \times 5 + (\text{length bin})
\]

\textbf{Example:} The answer will be correct (reward = 1) and there are 5 tokens left (bin = 2):
\[
\text{ZIP token} = 1 \times 5 + 2 = 7 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \ziptoken{7}
\]

\section{How Training Works}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{The Training Pipeline}

ZIP-RC training has four stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Generate}: Run the base model on problems to get completions
    \item \textbf{Score}: Check if each answer was correct (yes = 1, no = 0)
    \item \textbf{Build dataset}: Create training examples from every prefix position
    \item \textbf{Train}: Teach the model which ZIP token to predict at each position
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Key Insight: Prefix Training}

For a completion with T tokens, we create T training examples---one for each prefix.

\textbf{At each position:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input}: The prompt plus the first t tokens of the completion
    \item \textbf{Target}: The ZIP token that describes (final correctness, tokens remaining)
\end{itemize}

This teaches the model to ``see the future''---predicting the outcome at every step.

\subsection{Worked Example}

Let's walk through a complete example:

\textbf{Setup:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Question: ``What is 15 + 27?''
    \item Model generates: ``15 + 27 = 42'' (11 tokens)
    \item Ground truth answer: 42
    \item Since 42 = 42, the reward is 1 (correct!)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Training examples created:}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}clccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Position} & \textbf{Prefix so far} & \textbf{Tokens left} & \textbf{Bin} & \textbf{Target} \\
\midrule
1 & ``15'' & 10 & 3 & \ziptoken{8} \\
4 & ``15 + 27'' & 7 & 2 & \ziptoken{7} \\
8 & ``15 + 27 = 4'' & 3 & 1 & \ziptoken{6} \\
11 & ``15 + 27 = 42'' & 0 & 0 & \ziptoken{5} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Notice: All targets are \ziptoken{5}--\ziptoken{9} because the final answer is correct (reward = 1).

\subsection{The Training Loss}

Training uses standard cross-entropy loss---nothing fancy:

The model outputs probabilities for all 10 ZIP tokens. We want it to assign high probability to the correct one. The loss is just:
\[
\text{Loss} = -\log(\text{probability of correct ZIP token})
\]

If the model predicts the right token with 90\% confidence, loss is low. If it's only 10\% confident, loss is high.

\section{How Inference Works}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Zero Overhead---The Clever Part}

Here's the magic: ZIP predictions cost \textbf{nothing extra} at inference time.

\textbf{How it works:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item ZIP tokens are added to the vocabulary (10 new entries at the end)
    \item The model's output layer produces logits for \textit{all} tokens, including ZIP tokens
    \item We just look at the last 10 logits to get our introspection predictions
    \item ZIP tokens are masked during generation (set to $-\infty$) so they're never actually output as text
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Key point:} No extra forward passes needed. The ZIP logits come out alongside regular token logits---for free.

\subsection{Getting Predictions from ZIP Logits}

After a forward pass, we have 10 numbers (the ZIP logits). Here's what to do with them:

\textbf{Step 1: Get probabilities}\\
Apply softmax to convert logits to probabilities. Now we have 10 probabilities that sum to 1.

\textbf{Step 2: Get success probability}\\
Add up the probabilities for the ``correct'' tokens (\ziptoken{5} through \ziptoken{9}):
\[
P(\text{correct}) = P(\ziptoken{5}) + P(\ziptoken{6}) + P(\ziptoken{7}) + P(\ziptoken{8}) + P(\ziptoken{9})
\]

\textbf{Step 3: Get length estimate}\\
Similarly, group by length bin to estimate remaining tokens.

\textbf{Example:} If the ZIP probabilities are heavily concentrated on \ziptoken{7} (correct, 4--7 tokens left), you know the model is confident it's on the right track with several tokens to go.

\section{Architecture Overview}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{The ZipRCModel Wrapper}

The \texttt{ZipRCModel} class wraps any standard language model:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
class ZipRCModel:
    def __init__(self, base_model):
        # Add 10 ZIP tokens to vocabulary
        tokenizer.add_tokens(['<ZIP_0>', ..., '<ZIP_9>'])
        model.resize_token_embeddings(...)

    def forward(self, input_ids):
        # Get logits from base model (includes ZIP tokens)
        logits = self.model(input_ids)

        # Split the output
        text_logits = logits[:, :-10]   # Regular tokens
        zip_logits = logits[:, -10:]    # ZIP predictions

        # For generation: mask ZIP tokens so they can't be sampled
        gen_logits = logits.clone()
        gen_logits[:, -10:] = -infinity

        return text_logits, gen_logits, zip_logits
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{What Each Output Is For}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{text\_logits}: Computing probabilities over real text tokens
    \item \textbf{gen\_logits}: Actually generating text (ZIP tokens masked out)
    \item \textbf{zip\_logits}: Getting introspection predictions
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Current Limitations}

\textbf{Note:} The current implementation uses a bare ``Q:/A:'' prompt format. With this simple template, all examples may land in reward\_bin=0 (incorrect), because the base model often fails to produce correct answers without additional guidance.

\textbf{To get training signal for both reward classes}, you would need:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{More examples}: A larger and more diverse prompt dataset increases the chance of some correct answers
    \item \textbf{Few-shot prompting}: Include example Q\&A pairs before the actual question to guide the model
    \item \textbf{Chain-of-thought prompting}: Encourage the model to show its reasoning step-by-step, which often improves accuracy
    \item \textbf{Easier problems}: Start with simpler math problems where the base model is more likely to succeed
\end{itemize}

Having both correct and incorrect rollouts in the training data is important---it teaches the model to distinguish between paths that lead to success versus failure.

\section{Practical Applications}
%==============================================================================

Once you have a trained ZIP-RC model, here's what you can do with it:

\subsection{Early Stopping}

If the model thinks it's going to fail, stop early:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
if probability_of_success < 0.3:
    stop_and_try_different_approach()
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Smart Beam Search}

When exploring multiple paths, score them by expected utility:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
for each beam:
    score = P(success) - cost_weight * expected_length
keep the top-k beams by score
\end{lstlisting}

This automatically prunes paths that are both unlikely to succeed AND expensive.

\subsection{Best-of-N Selection}

Generate N completions, pick the one most likely to be correct:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
completions = [generate() for _ in range(N)]
best = pick completion with highest P(success)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Uncertainty Detection}

If the ZIP probabilities are spread across many tokens (high entropy), the model is uncertain. This is a signal to explore multiple paths:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
if model_is_uncertain:
    branch_into_multiple_paths()
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Summary of Benefits}

\begin{description}
    \item[Zero Overhead:] No extra computation---introspection is free
    \item[Real-time:] Predictions available at every token
    \item[Joint Modeling:] Captures the relationship between success and length
    \item[Plug and Play:] Works with any causal language model
\end{description}

\section{Summary}
%==============================================================================

ZIP-RC is a simple framework that gives language models self-awareness during generation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Add 10 special tokens} that encode (reward, length) predictions
    \item \textbf{Create training data} by labeling every prefix of every completion
    \item \textbf{Train} the model to predict the right ZIP token at each position
    \item \textbf{Use} the ZIP predictions to make smarter decisions during inference
\end{enumerate}

The key insight is that by training on prefix-level labels, the model learns to predict outcomes at \textit{every step} of generation. And because ZIP logits come from the same forward pass as regular token logits, this introspection is completely free.
